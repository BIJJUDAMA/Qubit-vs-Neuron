\section{Discussion: Classical vs Quantum Learning Frontiers}\label{sec:frontiers}

\subsection{Why Classical Neural Networks Overwhelmingly Outperform QNNs on Classical Data}
Our empirical findings across Iris, Two Moons, and Concentric Circles align with foundational principles in learning theory, optimization, and physics:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Activation functions and non-unitary dynamics:} Classical deep networks compose unconstrained, continuous non-linear activations such as $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$. ReLU units partition the Euclidean feature space $\mathbb{R}^d$ into polyhedral linear regions whose boundary complexity grows exponentially with network depth without sacrificing gradient propagation. In stark contrast, closed quantum evolution is strictly unitary: $|\psi'\rangle = U|\psi\rangle$. Unitary transformations are linear isometric operators on the complex Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ that preserve inner products and $L_2$ state norms. Non-linearity enters a VQC solely through measurement expectations $\langle \psi | \mathcal{O} | \psi \rangle$ and periodic trigonometric functions generated by Pauli rotations ($e^{-i \theta \sigma}$). This restricts QNNs to smooth, truncated Fourier series, rendering piecewise-sharp decision boundaries (such as the narrow crescent cusps in Two Moons) costly to approximate without high circuit depth.
  \item \textbf{The power of overparameterization without trainability collapse:} In classical deep learning, overparameterization (networks with millions of parameters) smooths the loss landscape, eliminates spurious local minima, and accelerates gradient descent convergence via the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime. Conversely, overparameterization in quantum variational circuits induces severe expressivity bottlenecks: as the ansatz approaches a 2-design (universal Haar distribution), the gradient variance vanishes exponentially with register size ($\text{Var}[\partial_\theta \mathcal{L}] \in \mathcal{O}(2^{-n})$), a disaster known as \emph{barren plateaus} [5]. Thus, classical models can scale parameters unrestrictedly, whereas QNNs must navigate a razor's edge between limited expressive power and untrainable barren landscapes.
  \item \textbf{Hardware execution and backpropagation efficiency:} On classical CPUs and GPUs, reverse-mode automatic differentiation backpropagates gradients through millions of parameters in a single forward-backward pass ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ overhead relative to inference). On quantum processors, analytic gradient evaluation relies on the parameter-shift rule:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \langle \mathcal{O} \rangle}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{\langle \mathcal{O} \rangle_{\theta_j + \frac{\pi}{2}} - \langle \mathcal{O} \rangle_{\theta_j - \frac{\pi}{2}}}{2},
  \end{equation}
  demanding $2P$ distinct circuit evaluations for $P$ parameters. In the presence of finite-shot shot noise, obtaining $\epsilon$-accurate gradients requires $\mathcal{O}(P / \epsilon^2)$ shots, introducing debilitating wall-clock and sample complexity for training on quantum hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Four Problem Regimes Where Quantum Neural Networks Win}
Despite their disadvantages on generic classical tabular vectors, quantum models hold mathematically proven and empirically validated niches:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Quantum-Native Data (States from Physical Systems):}
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{The Problem:} Learning properties, invariants, or classification labels of quantum states directly produced by quantum sensors, quantum repeaters, condensed-matter simulations, or physical quantum computers.
    \item \emph{Why QNNs Win:} Classical networks suffer an exponential measurement bottleneck: they must first perform quantum state tomography (requiring millions of physical measurements) to convert the quantum density matrix into classical numbers before training can begin. A QNN can accept the quantum state $|\psi_{\text{data}}\rangle$ directly into its register via coherent quantum access ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ state transfer), bypassing tomography entirely [7].
    \item \emph{Examples:} Detecting quantum phase transitions in transverse-field Ising or Heisenberg models, classification of topological order, and quantum error correction syndrome decoding. The open-access \emph{QDataSet} repository [6] provides standardized quantum state and dynamics benchmarks specifically for evaluating algorithms in this native regime.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Discrete Logarithm and Cryptographic / Group-Theoretic Distributions:}
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{The Problem:} Classifying data points sampled from algebraic groups related to discrete logarithms, hidden subgroups, or modular exponentiation.
    \item \emph{Why QNNs Win:} Liu et al. mathematically proved a rigorous, exponential separation in supervised learning [8]: a quantum kernel classifier can classify data generated by discrete log problems in polynomial time ($\mathcal{O}(\text{poly}(n))$), whereas no classical polynomial-time algorithm can achieve classification accuracy better than random guessing unless standard cryptographic hardness assumptions fail (i.e., discrete log is solvable in classical polynomial time, BPP).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{High-Order Parity and Correlated XOR Problems:}
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{The Problem:} Learning $k$-parity classification functions ($y = x_{i_1} \oplus x_{i_2} \oplus \cdots \oplus x_{i_k}$) in high dimensions.
    \item \emph{Why QNNs Win:} Classical MLPs suffer from flat loss plateaus and exponential sample complexity when learning parity functions without intermediate representation cues. Quantum feature maps with multi-qubit Pauli-$Z$ parity measurements naturally evaluate the parity Hamiltonian in $\mathcal{O}(1)$ quantum circuit depth using entangling gates [9].
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Quantum Kernels with Hard Classical Evaluation:}
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{The Problem:} Evaluating kernel classifiers with Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial-time (IQP) feature maps $U_\Phi(x)$.
    \item \emph{Why QNNs Win:} Estimating the inner product $K(x, x') = |\langle 0 | U_\Phi^\dagger(x) U_\Phi(x') | 0 \rangle|^2$ corresponds to computing transition probabilities of commuting quantum circuits that are provably classically $\#\mathbf{P}$-hard to simulate or sample [10]. If a learning problem's natural geometry aligns with this quantum kernel, classical neural networks cannot efficiently approximate the feature space.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Empirical Validation on Quantum Dynamics: The QDataSet Benchmark}
To evaluate model performance in a regime where data originates directly from physical quantum dynamics, we incorporated a standardized two-qubit Hamiltonian classification task from the open-source \emph{QDataSet} repository (Perrier, Youssry \& Ferrie, 2022 \cite{perrier2022qdataset}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Physical System and Dynamics:} A bipartite two-qubit register evolves under time-dependent Hamiltonian dynamics:
  \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}(t) = (\omega_1 Z \otimes I + \omega_2 I \otimes Z) + c_1(t) X \otimes I + c_2(t) I \otimes X + g_{\text{int}} X \otimes X.
  \end{equation}
  The task is to classify whether the physical register experienced unentangled separable dynamics ($g_{\text{int}} = 0$) or bipartite interacting dynamics ($g_{\text{int}} \in [0.8, 3.0]$) given observable Pauli expectation measurements $[\langle XI \rangle, \langle IX \rangle, \langle XX \rangle, \langle ZZ \rangle]$.
  \item \textbf{Empirical Findings:} On this physical quantum dynamics benchmark, the depth-2 QNN achieves \qdatasetHamiltonianQNNdTwoEntAccuracy{} test accuracy with only 13 trainable parameters, closely matching the capacity-matched compact MLP (\qdatasetHamiltonianMLPSmallAccuracy{}, paired difference $p = 0.265$) while the overparameterized wide MLP reaches \qdatasetHamiltonianMLPAccuracy{}. This demonstrates that in regimes where data reflects quantum unitary symmetries and non-local correlations, parameter-matched QNNs exhibit strong representation capability without suffering degradation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
This case study conducted a rigorous, reproducible comparison of variational quantum classifiers against classical multilayer perceptrons across four distinct geometric distributions: an overlapping, realistic \emph{Iris} task (\emph{Iris versicolor} vs. \emph{Iris virginica}), the nonlinear \emph{Two Moons} distribution, rotationally symmetric \emph{Concentric Circles}, and physical \emph{QDataSet} two-qubit Hamiltonian dynamics.

On standard Euclidean tabular distributions, classical MLPs consistently outperformed or matched QNNs, demonstrating superior expressivity, optimization smoothness, and wall-clock execution speed. However, on geometric tasks aligned with quantum inductive biases---such as rotationally symmetric Concentric Circles ($p < 0.001$) and physical quantum Hamiltonian dynamics---the variational quantum classifier effectively matched or exceeded capacity-matched classical networks with exceptionally compact parameter counts ($\le 13$ parameters).

Our analysis elucidates why classical neural networks will continue to dominate generic classical tabular applications, while delineating the four foundational frontiers---quantum-native data (formalized in QDataSet \cite{perrier2022qdataset}), group-theoretic kernel separations, high-order parity Hamiltonians, and hard quantum kernels---where quantum learning models offer unique physical and structural advantages. The entire experiment suite, test coverage, and automated compilation ensure end-to-end scientific provenance and one-command reproducibility.
